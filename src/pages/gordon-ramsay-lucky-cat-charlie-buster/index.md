Charlie Buster hand-painted 300 Lucky Cats for Gordon Ramsay’s global restaurant brand, Lucky Cat.

Black, gold, glossy and grimey, the cats were small fortune machines with attitude. Each one was painted by hand. Each one had its own slight differences. No two were exactly the same.

They were not just table decorations.

They were tiny art objects.

Then they started vanishing.

What began as a hand-painted restaurant art project became part of a strange real-life story around Gordon Ramsay’s Lucky Cat restaurants: customers taking the cats home, the figurines disappearing from tables, and the whole thing turning into the kind of detail that feels almost too odd not to belong in a Netflix documentary.

From Charlie’s paint-splattered table to Gordon Ramsay’s Lucky Cat, from restaurant interiors to the wider story around *Being Gordon Ramsay*, the cats took on a life of their own.

Hand-painted.\
Stolen.\
Recreated.\
Still waving.

## **The Lucky Cat Commission**

Charlie Buster was commissioned to hand-paint 300 Lucky Cats for Lucky Cat by Gordon Ramsay.

The brief matched Charlie’s world perfectly: take a familiar object and give it grime, movement, luxury and character. The Lucky Cat already carries symbolism. Traditionally known as a maneki-neko, the waving cat is associated with luck, fortune and welcome.

Charlie’s version pushed that idea into a more street-led visual space.

The cats were painted in black and gold, with a glossy finish and a grimey edge. They looked premium, but not clean in the boring sense. They had shine, attitude and texture. They felt part restaurant object, part art piece, part strange little mascot.

That balance is exactly where Charlie Buster’s Grime Art language works best.

Luxury, but disrupted.\
Gloss, but dirtied up.\
Restaurant design, but with street energy.\
A lucky charm turned into a miniature painted character.

Each cat was handled individually, painted by hand and finished with subtle differences. That made the job repetitive in scale, but personal in execution.

Three hundred cats.

Three hundred surfaces.

Three hundred chances to make something small feel alive.

## **From Studio Table to Gordon Ramsay’s Lucky Cat**

Before the cats reached the restaurants, they existed in the rawest possible setting: Charlie’s workspace.

Rows of blank cats. Paint. Black and gold. Gloss. Repetition. Drying time. Touch-ups. Small details. The kind of job that looks simple from the outside but requires focus, patience and consistency when you are doing hundreds by hand.

A project like this is not the same as one large canvas.

With a canvas, the energy can build across one surface. With 300 objects, the challenge is different. The artist has to keep the work consistent without making every piece feel dead and identical.

That is harder than it sounds.

Every cat had to feel like part of the same family. But every cat still needed a little life of its own.

That is where hand-painted work separates itself from mass production. A machine can repeat. A hand leaves traces.

Charlie’s Lucky Cats carried those traces.

## **Black, Gold and Grime**

The visual language of the cats was simple but strong.

Black gave them weight.\
Gold gave them fortune.\
Gloss gave them luxury.\
The grime gave them Charlie Buster.

That combination made sense for Lucky Cat. Gordon Ramsay’s restaurant brand already sits in a world of atmosphere, nightlife, Asian-inspired dining, polished interiors and high-end experience. The cats had to fit that environment, but they also needed enough edge to stand out.

Charlie’s approach gave them that.

They were not overcomplicated. They did not need to be. The point was impact. A small object on a restaurant table has to communicate quickly. It has to catch light, catch attention and make people want to pick it up.

That final part became the problem.

People did want to pick them up.

And some people did not put them back.

---

## **The Cat Heist**

The Lucky Cats became part of a real-life restaurant mystery: they started disappearing.

Gordon Ramsay later spoke publicly about Lucky Cat figurines being stolen from the restaurants, with reports claiming hundreds went missing in a single week. Some accounts around the story said multiple cats were taken in one night alone.

For Charlie Buster, that detail gave the project a strange afterlife.

Imagine painting hundreds of small artworks for one of the biggest chefs on earth, then watching the objects become part of what might be the poshest cat robbery in Europe.

It sounds ridiculous.

But it also makes complete sense.

The cats were small, collectable-looking, visually strong and connected to a Gordon Ramsay restaurant. They had the exact quality that makes people want to take something home as a trophy, even when they obviously should not.

That is the funny, awkward truth of it.

The cats worked too well.

They became memorable enough to steal.

## **Restaurant Objects Becoming Cultural Objects**

Most restaurant decorations are invisible.

People sit down, eat, drink, talk, take photos, pay the bill and leave. The objects around them blur into the background. A candle. A menu. A chopstick rest. A little figure on the table. Most of it is forgotten.

But sometimes an object becomes part of the story.

The Lucky Cats did that.

They were not just part of the décor. They became something people noticed, touched, photographed, talked about and, in too many cases, tried to leave with.

That shift matters because it shows the power of designed objects inside hospitality.

A restaurant is not only food. It is atmosphere, memory and detail. The best restaurants create worlds. Everything in the room contributes: lighting, sound, plates, staff, tables, art, objects and texture.

Charlie Buster’s cats became part of that world.

They helped build the Lucky Cat atmosphere, then escaped it.

Literally.

## **One Cat Reworked Into Wall Art**

One of the cats later found another life when it was reworked into wall art by Gabriella Anouk.

That detail adds another layer to the story.

The cats did not just disappear or sit still. At least one of them evolved into a new artwork. It moved from small object to wall piece, from restaurant mascot to reimagined art.

That is exactly how culture works when objects have enough energy in them.

They do not stay fixed.

They get reused, remixed, stolen, archived, photographed, talked about, damaged, saved or transformed.

The Lucky Cats became more than a commission because they kept moving after the job was done.

That movement is part of the appeal.

## **The Strange Value of Stolen Art Objects**

Nobody should steal restaurant property. That part is clear.

But culturally, stolen objects often reveal something interesting: people only steal what they notice.

The Lucky Cats were noticed.

They were small enough to take, strong enough to remember and desirable enough for people to risk looking ridiculous over. That tells you something about the object’s pull.

In a strange way, the thefts proved the cats had presence.

If they had been forgettable, they would have stayed where they were.

Instead, they became part of the story.

For an artist, that is surreal. You want the work to connect with people, but you do not usually expect that connection to involve someone smuggling it out of a Gordon Ramsay restaurant.

Still, it makes the project unforgettable.

And unforgettable is better than invisible.
