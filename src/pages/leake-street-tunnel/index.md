## What Is Leake Street Tunnel?

Leake Street Tunnel is a graffiti-covered tunnel beneath Waterloo Station in London. It is one of the most famous street art locations in the UK and is widely known as the Banksy Tunnel because of its connection to Banksy’s 2008 Cans Festival.

Unlike most public walls in London, Leake Street became known as a tolerated space for graffiti and street art. That made it a rare environment where artists, writers, tourists, photographers, collectors and culture hunters could all collide in one place.

The tunnel is constantly changing. A wall can look completely different from one week to the next. Tags, throw-ups, murals, stencils, characters, slogans, portraits and full productions all compete for space. Nothing is guaranteed to last. That is part of its power.

Leake Street is not a clean gallery. It is not white walls, polished floors and quiet collectors sipping wine. It is noise, spray paint, damp brick, railway arches, movement, pressure and history. It is London street culture in real time.

That is why it matters to Charlie Buster.

Grime Art was never designed to feel polished, safe or over-produced. It was built from the same energy that lives inside spaces like Leake Street: rough surfaces, bold marks, fast decisions, layered paint, music culture, street identity and controlled chaos.

## Charlie Buster and Graffiti Kings

Charlie Buster’s connection to graffiti culture runs through his work with Graffiti Kings London, one of the most recognisable names in UK graffiti and commercial street art. Through that world, Charlie developed a visual style that moved between raw graffiti foundations and more detailed, collector-focused artwork.

His work has been described as colourful, layered and detailed, often using spray paint, paint pens, stencil work and hand-drawn marks. These elements became part of what Charlie calls **Grime Art** — a raw British art form influenced by graffiti, UK grime culture, street art, pop culture and luxury one-of-one artwork.

Graffiti Kings matters because it gave Charlie a route into a professional graffiti environment while still keeping the street-level attitude of the culture. That balance is important. Grime Art is not just graffiti on a canvas, and it is not just music-inspired artwork. It sits between worlds.

It can exist on a tunnel ceiling.  
It can exist on a canvas.  
It can exist on a hand-painted military jacket.  
It can exist as a portrait of a grime artist, rapper, footballer, film character or cultural icon.

That flexibility is part of the movement.

Charlie Buster’s background gives Grime Art credibility because it did not come from a marketing meeting. It came from walls, commissions, tunnels, studios, street culture and years of developing a visual language in public.

Charlie’s BUSTER piece is not separate from Leake Street. It is now part of the Leake Street story.

## Why the Roof Matters

Painting a roof is different from painting a wall.

A wall invites eye-level attention. A ceiling piece has to fight harder. It has to work from awkward angles, survive poor lighting, sit above the viewer and still carry enough force to be noticed. It becomes something people look up to — literally.

At Leake Street, the roof has always carried a different kind of status. The walls shift rapidly. The ceiling feels harder to reach, harder to paint and harder to own visually. A roof piece has to be bold enough to hold its place above the chaos.

Charlie’s BUSTER piece does that through scale, simplicity and impact. It does not need to over-explain itself. It works like a street-level logo stamped into one of London’s most photographed graffiti environments.

This is where Charlie Buster’s Grime Art language starts to make sense.

Grime Art is not about making something neat. It is about making something felt. It is about texture, attitude, repetition, confidence and visual noise. It borrows the physical energy of graffiti and combines it with the emotional punch of grime music and British street culture.

A roof piece in Leake Street is exactly the kind of place where that language belongs.

## What Are “Busterisms”?

One of the most recognisable parts of Charlie Buster’s visual identity is his use of recurring marks, shapes and symbols — sometimes referred to as **Busterisms**.

These include zigzags, arrows, double arrows, sharp movement marks, layered outlines, punchy colour hits and repeated graphic gestures. They act almost like visual ad-libs. The same way a grime MC might bring a signature flow, reload, phrase or delivery pattern, Charlie brings repeated visual rhythms into his paintings.

That is one of the clearest links between Grime Art and grime music.

Grime music is fast, direct, raw and unmistakably British. It is built on energy, repetition, aggression, rhythm and personality. Grime Art takes that same feeling and turns it into paint.

The Busterisms are not random decoration. They are part of the identity system. They make the work recognisable across walls, canvases, jackets, portraits and murals.

A Charlie Buster piece does not have to say “Grime Art” to feel like Grime Art. The marks, colours, attitude and movement do the work.

## Leake Street as a Living Gallery

Leake Street is often called a graffiti tunnel, but it also works like a living gallery. The difference is that nobody fully controls it.

Traditional galleries decide who gets the wall, when the show opens, when the work comes down and what story gets told. Leake Street works differently. The space edits itself. Artists paint over artists. New work buries old work. Photographers document what they can before it disappears. The tunnel becomes an archive that constantly destroys and rebuilds itself.

That is why lasting presence in Leake Street carries weight.

A painting in that space has to earn attention in a brutal environment. It is surrounded by other work from serious writers, street artists, tourists, amateurs, professionals and international names. Nothing is protected. Nothing is sacred. The tunnel does not care about your CV.

This makes Charlie Buster’s roof presence more important. His BUSTER piece has not just appeared in a famous location. It has remained part of the tunnel’s visual ceiling through years of change.

That kind of survival is a form of credibility.

For collectors, this matters because provenance is not only about who owns a work. It is also about where the artist has left their mark. Leake Street gives Charlie Buster’s story a strong London graffiti anchor.

It is the visual version of that energy.

It takes the aggression of graffiti, the attitude of streetwear, the boldness of pop art, the detail of portrait work, the chaos of urban walls and the confidence of UK grime culture, then turns it into one-of-one artwork.

That is why Leake Street is such an important reference point. The tunnel already contains the ingredients of Grime Art: layers, speed, public reaction, visual competition, rebellion, humour, style and pressure.

Charlie Buster did not have to invent those ingredients from nothing. He lived around them, painted through them and turned them into a more defined art form.
